Jean-Jacques Rousseau was a decisive voice of the eighteenth-century French Enlightenment—born in Geneva, writing mainly in French, across politics, education, morals, and literature.

In *The Social Contract* he holds that a truly legitimate order rests on popular sovereignty: the people are not an appendage of the state but the sovereign of the political community. Through the social contract they unite and make public rules on the basis of the common interest. He stresses the “general will”—not a mere sum of private wills, nor every majority vote by nature—but the law members ought to share when they think from the whole common good. Freedom is then not only the absence of constraint, but taking part, as members, in making the laws one obeys.

Rousseau also warns that if power claims to speak for the general will while silencing dissent, that will can be usurped. His thought advances debate on popular sovereignty and participation, and keeps later readers asking how to stop rule “in the people’s name” from ruling against the people.
