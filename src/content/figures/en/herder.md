The herder is the mobile-flock branch of agriculture: cattle and sheep (and in some regions horses or camels) on natural or semi-natural pasture—not a single biography. Unlike the pen livestock raiser, the feed axis is grass and movement, not a concentrate bucket; capital sits in the flock and the route; boundaries are often shared pasture and seasonal transfer. One blizzard, drought, or overgraze can empty grass and animals together.

Information stays on the road: which slope greens, which valley still holds water, which buyer cuts the price. Without stocking-balance and vaccine ledgers, experience walks with the old herder while the commons thins under the private rational of “one more head”—the same open-access logic as coastal capture.

## Historical limits

The constraint is structural. On open pasture, households add animals; the collective result is overgraze. Weak winter feed and shelter make extreme weather lethal. Markets are distant; exit depends on long drives or middlemen. Oral route lore does not settle into enforceable stocking rates and rest periods.

## Personal and role limits

These are limits of the type. Skill is bound to reading grass and animals, driving, and night watch. That does not convert easily into range science or meat-cutting plant management. Spare capital is thin; blizzard or plague can empty a household. In this gallery the pen raiser stakes feed money; the herder stakes whether tomorrow’s grass is still there.

## A modern path

The point is not endless heads. It is to turn shared pasture into governed stocking balance.

Rates and rest: village or cooperative quotas, rotation, and winter forage stores. Unified biosecurity and exit inspection tied to slaughter specs. Traceable lots: ear tags or flock IDs, moves and dosing on the books; information kept with herding organizations instead of nameless long-haul supply.
