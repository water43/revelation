The vegetable grower is the “fresh” branch of planting: leaf, fruiting, and root crops for the town table—not a single biography. Fields sit nearer the city or the road; labor is denser; rotations turn faster. Like fish, the crop fears the night: delay means yellow leaves, water loss, and a cut price. Against the staple crop farmer—grain can dry and store—the vegetable window is counted in hours. Same structure as the Newhaven fishwife: the person is cold chain and last mile; only the source differs—bed or sea.

Information stays oral: which wholesaler cuts, which street buys, whether tomorrow’s rain ruins the cut. Without pre-orders and graded crates, the household gambles the dawn market.

## Historical limits

The constraint is structural. Fresh greens will not keep; urban wholesale power concentrates; the field edge bargains weakly. When residue and appearance standards arrive late, returns and price cuts land on the grower. Fragmented plots make a shared harvest day hard. Oral market talk does not settle into a dispatchable sowing plan.

## Personal and role limits

These are limits of the type. Skill is bound to reading rotations, dawn stamina, and bargaining. That does not convert easily into a wash-and-cut plant or city dispatch. Spare capital is thin; one storm or glut can empty the season’s stake. In this gallery the staple farmer stakes the year; the vegetable grower stakes every dawn.

## A modern path

The point is not more market runs. It is to turn greens into bookable, graded goods.

Orders first: community groups, restaurants, or retail pre-orders set sowing and cut days. Field-edge chill and grading: crates by size to stretch the sellable window. Light processing in the village so what leaves is transportable; price and waste data on the cooperative ledger, instead of nameless wholesale supply.
