The urban IT worker is an occupational type from mid-twentieth-century electronic data processing (EDP) rooms in cities, not a single biography. White shirt, raised floor, tape and punched cards, console—this became the standard look. Operators loaded tape and cards; programmers wrote code; systems analysts broke down the business. Together they turned accounts, inventory, payroll, and scheduling into jobs that could be run again. Today’s software engineers, operators, and implementers in office towers continue the same line of work. The product is a reproducible information process, not a perishable catch that must be sold the same day like the Newhaven fishwife’s fish.

Information can be copied, stored, and standardized—card columns, record formats, job-control language. Cities supplied steady power, mainframes, and organizational budgets, which made informatization possible. Ford and Taylor dealt in stockable materials; the IT worker deals in stockable transactions and rules. The work sits inside the firm or the state, not on a haul-and-resell chain from shore to street.

What the front-line person still sells is hours and skill. Machines, platforms, and customer data usually belong to the organization. Later software engineering and continuous integration industrialized coding itself; most of the surplus still lands with organizations and platforms, not with the individual “machine-room hand.” Glass-walled computer rooms, white shirts, and tape cabinets became the visual sign of urban information labor, as the creel was for the village fish seller.
