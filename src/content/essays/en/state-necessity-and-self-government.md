This essay asks why states appear, how state power can be legitimate, and whether people can govern themselves within a public order. Early states often drew recognition through religion and sacrifice; the high priest is a classic figure at the meeting point of sacred mediation and public order. What follows moves from historical formation to political legitimacy, then to Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and modern institutional arrangements.

## 1. Why does the state appear?

The state is not an organizational form that human society has always had.

In early human life, society was organized mainly through households, clans, tribes, and tribal leagues. As agriculture developed, populations grew, and settled life spread, relations among people became more complex, and new problems appeared:

- How should land, water, and other resources be allocated?
- Who adjudicates when groups conflict?
- How can large-scale production, construction, and defense be organized?
- How are common rules made, and how are they enforced?
- How is overall order kept when members’ interests collide?

These problems gradually exceeded what a single household or tribe could handle.

More stable public authorities then emerged. They organized production, allocated resources, made rules, settled disputes, collected taxes, and organized military force.

When such power became institutionalized—with relatively stable territory, population, and administration—the state began to take shape.

The rise of the state can thus be understood as a political form that human societies developed, after growing in scale and complexity, in order to organize common life.

Two questions must be kept apart:

Why the state appeared in history is a historical question; why the state has the right to rule the people is a question of political legitimacy.

That the state has historical causes does not mean any state power is naturally legitimate.

## 2. Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia: how early states formed

There was no single path to the early state.

Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia show two representative historical routes.

### 2.1 Ancient Egypt: a unified state along the Nile

Egyptian civilization grew with the Nile.

Annual floods gave fertile soil and a relatively stable agrarian system along the river.

As population and settlement grew, Upper and Lower Egypt formed distinct political centers, then gradually moved toward unification.

Around 3100 BCE, Egypt’s early dynastic state took shape. The Narmer Palette is often read as a key emblem of that unification.

The unified state developed:

- a political power centered on the pharaoh;
- administration for taxes, grain, and labor;
- organization for agriculture and public works;
- written records for administrative business;
- a legitimacy system linking pharaonic power to religious ideas.

In Egypt the pharaoh was not only a political ruler but also a religious figure.

Early states were not held by force alone; religion, tradition, and shared belief also helped the order gain social recognition.

### 2.2 Mesopotamia: competition among city-states

Unlike Egypt’s earlier unification, Mesopotamia long held many competing city-states—

Uruk, Ur, Lagash, and others—

competing over land, water, trade, and political influence.

Temples were central in early urban life: religious centers that also shaped land, grain, labor, and social management.

As competition and war grew, military rulers, administration, and law matured.

Some city-states and kingdoms later expanded into larger political systems.

### 2.3 What the two models show

Egypt unified earlier; Mesopotamia long remained a landscape of many city-states.

The state does not arise along one fixed route.

Geography, agriculture, population, war, trade, religion, and political rivalry all shape how states form.

The state is an institution formed over long social development—not something one person suddenly invented.

## 3. Without a state, can humans govern themselves?

This is a central question in political theory of the state.

If people can manage society through negotiation, mutual aid, and common rules, is a state with coercive power still needed?

Anarchist thought answers differently from classical state theory.

Anarchism is not simply chaos, nor license for anyone to act at will.

It stresses that order need not rest on a central authority standing above society.

People may handle public affairs through voluntary association, mutual-aid organizations, local self-government, and common deliberation.

For example:

- communities can set rules for shared space;
- residents can settle everyday problems by negotiation;
- workers can form cooperatives;
- local commons can be managed by the community itself.

This shows that human society is not wholly unable to organize without a state.

Yet hard questions remain:

Who acts if someone gravely violates others’ rights?

Who adjudicates conflicts between communities?

Who coordinates infrastructure that crosses regions?

If one organization holds vast resources and tries to force others to obey, how do others respond?

These questions do not prove self-rule impossible; they show that self-rule needs institutions, rules, and coordination.

The real issue is not a crude choice between state and no-state, but:

Can humans build a political order that has public organizational capacity without letting public power escape popular control?

## 4. Hobbes: the state must first solve security

In *Leviathan*, Thomas Hobbes gave a systematic account of why the state is necessary.

He offered a famous thought experiment: the state of nature—

the relations people may fall into when no common authority can effectively settle disputes and enforce rules.

Hobbes held that humans are equal in a certain sense:

not that capacities are identical, but that even the weaker may harm the stronger through alliance, design, or other means.

In seeking resources, safety, and honor, people may generate three main conflicts:

1. **Competition**: for gain, people may try to dominate or displace others.
2. **Diffidence**: unsure whether others will harm them, people may strike first.
3. **Glory**: to defend status and dignity, people may fight over insult or slight.

Without a common authority to constrain such conduct, insecurity can become continuous.

Hobbes therefore argued that people must, by covenant, hand some power to a common public authority—

to make rules, settle disputes, and keep order.

The state becomes an institution for security.

In short:

Without common authority, society may be unable to settle conflict stably; for security, people need a state.

But Hobbes leaves a hard question:

If the state itself becomes a force that harms the people, who constrains the state?

If the state holds vast power without effective oversight, have people only traded mutual fear for fear of the ruler?

That question leads to Locke.

## 5. Locke: state power must be limited

John Locke also wrote of the state of nature and social contract, but from a different starting point.

For Locke, the state of nature need not mean perpetual war of all against all.

People already hold basic rights there, such as:

- life;
- liberty;
- property.

These rights are not gifts of the state.

The state is established to protect rights that already belong to persons.

Why then create government at all?

Locke saw problems in the state of nature:

First, a lack of clear, commonly recognized legal standards.

Second, each person may be judge in their own cause, inviting partiality.

Third, a lack of sufficient public force to execute judgments.

People therefore form a political society and entrust some power to government—to make law, settle disputes, and protect rights.

That grant is not an unconditional surrender of all rights.

Government’s power comes from the people; its end is to protect the people.

So government must be limited by law and institutions.

If government betrays that end and violates basic rights, the people may resist it—even alter or remove it.

Locke marks a major shift:

The state is no longer only an authority that demands obedience; it is a public body accountable to the people.

Its existence needs popular authorization; its exercise must be constrained.

## 6. Rousseau: the people are not only authorizers but creators of order

If Hobbes asks how the state secures safety, and Locke how it protects rights, Rousseau asks further:

Who ought to decide the common rules of a political community?

In *The Social Contract*, Rousseau holds that a truly legitimate order rests on popular sovereignty.

The people are not an appendage of the state, nor mere objects of administration.

The people themselves are the sovereign of the political community.

Through the social contract they unite as a community and make public rules on the basis of the common interest.

He introduces a key idea: the general will.

The general will is not a mere sum of private opinions, nor is every majority vote automatically the general will.

It is members thinking, from the standpoint of the whole common interest, about rules all must share.

If a community’s rules serve only one group, they are hard to call truly public.

If rules form on the basis of common interest and apply to all members, they come closer to what Rousseau means by the general will.

Hence:

The people should be makers of law, not mere subjects of it.

Freedom is not only the absence of constraint.

It also means taking part, as members of the community, in making the laws one obeys—

a political freedom.

### The general will can be usurped

A warning is needed.

If a government, party, or ruling group claims to speak for the general will, how can the people tell whether it truly speaks for the common interest?

If dissent is branded as opposing the people or the community, the general will can become a tool of repression.

An institution’s claim to represent the people does not by itself prove popular sovereignty.

Whether people truly take part in rule-making, can voice dissent, and can oversee public power—those are the tests.

## 7. Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau: how the three combine

Their theories are not wholly exclusive.

They answer three central questions of modern political order.

| Thinker | Core question | Main claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hobbes | Why a state? | The state must provide security and public order |
| Locke | Whence state power? | Power comes from popular authorization and is limited by rights and law |
| Rousseau | Who decides common rules? | The people are sovereign and should take part in making public rules |

Together they yield a fuller logic:

First, humans need public organizational capacity to handle conflict, secure safety, and keep order.

Second, public power must not expand without limit. Government must be bound by law and institutions; its end should be the protection of basic rights.

Third, the people must not be only objects of management. They should take part in public affairs, make common rules, and oversee government.

A legitimate order needs both governing capacity and popular constraint on state power.

## 8. Can the people govern themselves?

A further question follows:

Even if the state is necessary, must every public matter be decided by the central government?

Clearly not.

Other thinkers studied how people manage their own affairs outside the state, or within state institutions, through local self-government and social organizations.

### 8.1 Tocqueville: local self-government and civic participation

In *Democracy in America*, Alexis de Tocqueville observed American local self-government and association.

He found many local public bodies.

Residents could take part in public affairs through local self-rule and handle shared problems through voluntary associations.

Such participation not only solved practical tasks; it formed civic awareness and capacity for self-rule.

Tocqueville worried especially that:

If every public matter is decided by central power, people may lose the capacity to take part in public life—

waiting for government instead of organizing and solving problems themselves.

Local self-government and association are therefore not only administrative devices; they are mechanisms by which democratic societies cultivate citizens.

### 8.2 Ostrom: commons can be managed by communities

Elinor Ostrom, through extensive field work, challenged a long-standing view:

that common resources, without strict central control or private property, must be destroyed.

She studied irrigation, fisheries, forests, groundwater, and similar commons.

Many local communities, she found, could manage shared resources for long periods under rules they made themselves.

Such self-rule is not rule-less.

Successful self-governance usually needs institutional conditions:

- clear boundaries of members and resource use;
- participation by those affected in making rules;
- monitoring;
- graduated sanctions for violations;
- low-cost dispute resolution;
- recognition of local organizations;
- nested coordination for large-scale commons.

Ostrom shows:

Popular self-government is not a fantasy. With suitable institutions, members can jointly manage public affairs within a range.

The state need not run everything.

It can coordinate at larger scales and keep public order, while communities, associations, and individuals exercise self-rule in their domains.

## 9. Why modern state institutions differ so widely

With those ideas in view, modern states look alike as forms of statehood yet differ sharply in the source of power, structure, and practice.

Several easily confused notions must be separated.

### 9.1 Form of state: how is the head of state produced?

Common types:

- **Monarchy**: the head of state is typically hereditary.
- **Republic**: the head of state is not a hereditary monarch.

A republic is not automatically a democracy.

A country without a hereditary monarch may still lack real political participation and oversight of power.

### 9.2 Form of government: how is executive power organized?

Common types include:

- presidential;
- parliamentary;
- semi-presidential.

In presidential systems the president is usually both head of state and head of government.

In parliamentary systems the government usually rests on a parliamentary majority, and the head of government is typically a prime minister.

Semi-presidential systems have both a president and a prime minister, with executive power allocated by constitutional rules.

### 9.3 Center and locality: how is power divided?

Common types include:

- federal;
- unitary.

Federal systems usually protect the division of power between the federation and its members in the constitution.

Unitary systems take central state power as the whole base; local power runs mainly under national law and institutional design.

Unitary does not mean every local matter must be handled directly by the center. Unitary states can still have substantial local self-government.

### 9.4 Actual political practice: can the people take part and oversee?

This is another dimension.

Even with a republic, presidency, or parliament, one cannot judge democratic depth by labels alone.

One must also ask:

- whether political competition is real;
- whether citizens can speak freely;
- whether government faces effective oversight;
- whether the judiciary is independent;
- whether succession of power is institutionally constrained;
- whether citizens can shape public policy through institutional channels.

Judging a political system requires looking at both constitutional structure and actual power in practice.

### 9.5 A few modern examples

| Country | Form of state | Form of government | Center–locality |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | Republic | Presidential | Federal |
| Germany | Republic | Parliamentary | Federal |
| France | Republic | Semi-presidential | Unitary |
| United Kingdom | Constitutional monarchy | Parliamentary | Unitary, with devolution |
| China | Republic | Institutional system under the people’s congresses | Unitary |
| Saudi Arabia | Monarchy | Administration under monarchy | Unitary |
| Iran | Republic combined with religious authority | Elected presidency and parliament alongside the supreme leader | Unitary |

These differences reflect different arrangements of political power, state organization, and public governance.

Emphatically:

Institutional names cannot replace analysis of actual political practice.

Elections do not guarantee effective oversight of all power; local governments do not guarantee ample local autonomy.

## 10. Three intellectual threads behind modern institutions

Taken together, three threads run through modern political institutions.

### First: Hobbes—the state must be able to keep order

Society cannot remain unable to settle conflict or protect basic security.

Public power, law, administration, and courts are needed for common order—

a question of governing capacity.

### Second: Locke—state power must be constrained

Public duties do not confer unlimited power.

Government must be bound by law and protect basic rights—

a question of legitimacy and the bounds of power.

### Third: Rousseau—the people must be masters of the political community

The people must not only receive management passively.

They should take part in making public rules and oversee government through institutions—

a question of popular sovereignty and political participation.

Together they pose:

How does the state keep order? How is power constrained? How do the people take part?

The threads complement and can conflict.

Government may expand power in the name of security; it may restrict some public action in the name of rights; majority decisions may harm minority rights.

Modern institutions try, through law, separation of powers, participation, judicial oversight, local self-government, and civil society, to reconcile these demands as far as possible.

## 11. State and people: from the administered to co-governors

Back to the opening question:

Why does the state exist at all?

Its appearance answers a institutional need for public organizational capacity after society grows complex.

It helps with problems individuals cannot solve alone: public safety, infrastructure, cross-regional coordination, dispute settlement, and shared-resource management.

But the state’s existence does not mean the people must hand every public matter to government—

nor that government may, in the state’s name, demand unconditional obedience.

A political order worth seeking needs several conditions at once:

First, necessary public governing capacity—

to keep order, handle public business, and secure basic safety.

Second, institutional constraints on state power—

government must not stand above the law, nor turn public power into private power for a few.

Third, real channels of political participation—

people should not only speak, but take part through institutions in making public rules and overseeing government.

Fourth, a measure of social self-governing capacity—

communities, local bodies, trades, and other associations can handle public affairs within a proper range.

Fifth, public rules that can be discussed, revised, and reexamined—

society is not static; needs change. A healthy order cannot treat existing institutions as forever fixed.

On this view, state and people should not be read as a simple manager–managed relation.

The state is one institutional form for organizing public life; the people are the foundation of the political community.

Government’s power should come from popular authorization; its duty should be to keep the order of common life; the people should keep the capacity to take part, oversee, and change public institutions.

The necessity of the state must not become a reason for unlimited expansion of state power; the freedom of the people must not be read as needing no public order at all.
